Getting started

Disjointed manual

Disjointed is a 2D planar mechanism simulator. You draw rigid bodies, place joints on them, couple the joints with pins, grounds, rails and sliders, then switch to Simulate mode and drag anything to see the whole linkage move.

This manual opens beside the canvas. In the app, click the help button (or press ?) to open it; while it is open, click any control to jump to its topic. F1 brings you back to this table of contents.

    Tutorial: your first mechanism

    A four-bar linkage in five steps. Every step uses a single tool; each tool disarms itself after one use.

    1. Draw three bars. Press B (Body) and click four corners for a long, thin rectangle; click the first corner again or press Enter to close it. Repeat for two more bars. Select a bar and press ] a few times to round its ends.
    2. Add joints. Press J (Joint) and click near each end of every bar. A joint belongs to the body it is placed on.
    3. Pin the bars together. Press C (Connect), click a joint on one bar, then a joint on the next. The second joint snaps onto the first. Do this twice to make a chain of three bars.
    4. Ground the two free ends. Press G (Ground) and click the joint at each end of the chain. A grounded joint stays where it is but still turns.
    5. Simulate. Press Tab to switch to Simulate mode and drag the middle bar. The whole linkage follows.

    Right-drag pans the view, the mouse wheel zooms, F fits the mechanism to the screen.

    The canvas

    The canvas is the drawing. What a click does depends on the mode and on the armed tool; with no tool armed, clicking selects and dragging moves. The hint at the right end of the toolbar always says what the next click will do.

    • Pan: drag with the right mouse button.
    • Zoom: mouse wheel, about the pointer.
    • Fit: F, or the fit button in the view group.
    • Cancel: Esc drops a half-drawn shape, disarms the tool, or clears the selection.

    Draw and Simulate modes Tab toggles

    The two buttons at the left of the toolbar switch between building the mechanism and running it. Entering Simulate settles every constraint once; leaving it puts every body back exactly where you drew it.

    Draw mode

    Build the mechanism: draw bodies, place joints, add constraints and dimensions, arrange and edit everything. Bodies move freely when dragged, and the sketch solver keeps any dimensions and constraints you added satisfied while you edit. Hold Shift while dragging to move a body rigidly with its linked neighbours instead.

    Simulate mode

    Drag any joint, or any point on a body, and the constraint solver propagates the motion through everything pinned, grounded or sliding with it. Nothing you do here changes the drawing: switching back to Draw mode restores the drawn layout. Press Space or the play button to run actuators and motors.

    Drawing tools

    The tool group

    Every drawing tool is one-shot: arm it with its button or letter key, use it once, and you are back in select mode. Each tool's letter is in its tooltip and in its topic heading below. Hovering a button shows a short description of what the tool wants you to click.

    Body B

    Draw a rigid body as a polygon: click each corner, then click the first corner again or press Enter to close. Clicks that land on existing joints or corners are remembered and turned into coincident constraints, so a body drawn between two joints stays attached to them.

    Start the body on existing free joints instead and it is built around them: the joints become its joints, expanded outward by a small margin. Corners can be rounded afterwards with [ and ], or by dragging a corner's fillet handle; Ctrl-drag rounds every corner at once.

    Hole U

    Cut a hole in a body. Click inside the body to start, then more vertices, and close on the first vertex or with Enter. For a round hole, press inside the body and drag out the radius instead. Holes have the same corner handles and rounding as outlines, and a round hole can carry a diameter dimension.

    Split X

    Cut a body in two. Click a point on its outline, route the cut through the inside with more clicks, and click the outline again to finish. Joints stay with whichever half they land in. Combine is the inverse.

    Combine N

    Merge the selected bodies into one. They must overlap or share an edge; the first selected keeps its colour. Select several bodies with Ctrl+click or a box selection, then press the combine button.

    Joint J

    Add a joint point. Click on a body to attach the joint to it; joints on the same body move rigidly together. Click on empty space for a free joint, which can be grounded as an anchor or used to build a body around later. A joint placed on a rail becomes a rider on that rail.

    Connect (pin) C

    Pin two joints together so they share a position but rotate freely. Click the first joint, then the second; the second moves onto the first. Pins are the hinges of a linkage.

    Weld W

    Lock two overlapping bodies rigidly together at a point, with no relative rotation. Click where the bodies overlap. Click an existing pinned joint to toggle it between a pin and a weld.

    Ground G

    Fix a joint in the world: it keeps its position but still turns. Click a body or a group instead to fix it entirely. Click a grounded element again to release it. Grounds are sacred to the solver: they never move, so an assembly that cannot reach them is reported as impossible.

    Rail K

    Draw a track between two joints. Two joints on the same body make a moving rail that travels with that body; two free joints make a fixed track in the world. Joints placed on a rail ride along it like a pin in a slot. Rails draw as double-headed arrows.

    Slider S

    Build a complete prismatic joint in two clicks. Click the body that should slide where its travel starts, then click where the travel ends: the body now slides along the arrow without rotating. If the second click lands on another body, the track rides that body; otherwise it is fixed in the world.

    Click an existing rail to add a slider on it, or click a rider to toggle its rotation lock (a locked rider is a slider; an unlocked one is a pin in a slot).

    Guideline L

    Place an infinite construction line through two points. Points land on joints, corners and edges, and snapping prefers guidelines over the grid, so they are the quickest way to line things up. Drag a guideline's handles to re-aim it.

    Linear pattern I

    Repeat a hole or a joint along one or two directions. Click the hole or joint, then where the next instance goes; a second click adds a second direction and makes a grid. Count and spacing are edited on the labels drawn beside the pattern. Members that do not fit inside the body are flagged.

    Circular pattern Q

    Repeat a hole or a joint around a centre. Click the hole or joint, then the centre of rotation. Count, angle and whether the members rotate with their position are edited on the canvas labels.

    Constraints and dimensions

    Sketch constraints

    Constraints are geometric rules between edges, rails and points that the sketch solver keeps satisfied in Draw mode. Each one draws a small badge; the eye button in this group shows or hides all badges, and a badge can be selected and deleted like anything else. Implicit constraints are also offered while you drag: pause near a horizontal or vertical alignment and it snaps in.

    Coincident O

    What it does: two points are held at the same place, or a point is held somewhere on a line (an edge, rail or guideline), while everything else stays free. This is how a body corner stays attached to a joint, or a joint stays on an edge, as you edit around it. Bodies drawn onto existing points get coincident constraints automatically.

    To add one: click two points, or a point and a line. Its badge is a small circled dot at the shared point.

    Horizontal H

    What it does: keeps an edge or rail level, or two points at the same height, in world axes. The sketch solver re-levels it after every edit, so you can drag other corners without the line tilting. Dragging near level also offers it as an implicit constraint.

    To add one: click an edge or rail, or two points. Its badge is an H glyph on the line.

    Vertical V

    What it does: keeps an edge or rail upright, or two points on the same vertical line, in world axes. Like Horizontal, it holds through every later edit and is offered implicitly while dragging.

    To add one: click an edge or rail, or two points. Its badge is a V glyph on the line.

    Parallel P

    What it does: keeps two lines (edges or rails, on the same body or different ones) at the same angle without fixing that angle: turn one and the other follows.

    To add one: click the two lines. A matching pair of badges marks both.

    Perpendicular T

    What it does: keeps two lines (edges or rails) at a right angle to each other, whatever their absolute orientation. Useful for square corners that must stay square while the body is reshaped.

    To add one: click the two lines. A matching pair of badges marks both.

    Equal length E

    What it does: keeps two lines (edges or rails) the same length: stretch one and the other stretches with it. Combine it with a driving dimension on one of them to size both.

    To add one: click the two lines. A matching pair of badges marks both.

    Measure D

    Place a dimension: click two references (points or lines), then click where the value should sit. A first click on a round hole makes a diameter dimension; on a rounded corner's arc, a radius dimension. Dimensions placed in Simulate mode are live read-outs that follow the motion.

    Dimensions

    A dimension starts driven: it reports the current value. Double-click its label and type a number to make it driving: the geometry moves to match and stays there while you edit elsewhere. Clear the value to make it driven again. The eye button in the measure group hides all dimensions.

    Editing

    Selecting and editing

    • Select by clicking; Ctrl+click adds to the selection; drag a box from empty space to select everything inside.
    • Move by dragging a selected body or joint. Snapping follows the grid, guidelines and object snap.
    • Edit a shape: a selected body shows its corner handles; drag them, double-click an edge to add a corner, double-click a corner to remove it.
    • Feature selection: with one body selected, Shift+drag a box to select some of its corners, holes and joints and move, delete or copy them together.
    • Delete with Delete or Backspace; undo with Ctrl+Z, redo with Ctrl+Y.
    • Copy and paste with Ctrl+C / Ctrl+V: a body travels with its joints, internal constraints and dimensions; the paste lands at the pointer.
    • Group with Ctrl+G so several bodies select and move as one; press it again on a group to dissolve it.

    Rotate R

    Turn the selected body about its centroid, or grab one of its nodes to turn it about that node. The angle snaps to multiples of 45° near them. With several bodies selected the whole set turns together.

    Mirror

    Flip the selection left-right or top-bottom in place, about the selection's own centre. Joints and constraints inside the selection mirror with it.

    Stacking order PageUp / PageDown

    Bring the selected body in front of everything else, or send it behind. Purely visual.

    Body colour

    With a body selected, the colour swatch recolours it. With nothing selected, it sets the colour new bodies get.

    Working units

    One world unit is one of the chosen unit (millimetres by default). Changing the unit moves nothing; it only changes how the grid, dimensions and exports interpret the numbers.

    Components

    Components

    A component packs a selection into a reusable definition and replaces it with an instance. Every instance follows its definition: edit one and all of them change. With an instance selected, the button forks it into a new independent component; with nothing selected it creates an empty component and opens it for editing. Instances can be mirrored and rotated like bodies.

    Component browser

    Lists every definition in the document. From here you insert an instance (click the canvas to place it), rename, edit or delete a definition. Hovering a row highlights its instances on the canvas.

    Editing a definition in context

    Double-click an instance to open its definition; all normal tools work inside. A breadcrumb at the top left shows where you are and takes you back out. Ctrl+double-click opens the definition with the whole enclosing assembly drawn faded around it: this context ghost is inert, but you can snap to it and place temporary dimensions onto it to position the definition against its surroundings. The eye on each breadcrumb chooses how many enclosing levels are shown.

    Simulation

    Running the animation Space

    In Simulate mode the play button drives every linear actuator and motor at once. Pausing and playing resumes smoothly from the current pose. The warning-triangle button next to it pauses the animation automatically when the assembly becomes impossible.

    Linear actuator A

    Click a slider or rail to make it self-driving: its carriage travels back and forth between the rail's ends during animation. Select it to set the speed and the motion profile (triangle for constant speed, sine for a smooth ease at the ends).

    Motor M

    Pick a pivot joint and then a crank pin on the same body: in animation the crank pin orbits the pivot at the configured angular speed. Select the motor to set its speed.

    Impossible assemblies

    When the constraints cannot all be satisfied at once (a link too short to reach its ground, for example), the solver keeps the grounds fixed, isolates the pins and sliders it cannot close, and still solves the rest. The breaks are drawn as red dotted lines and a banner appears at the top of the canvas.

    Solver tuning

    The sliders in the mode group adjust how hard the simulation works each frame: iterations per animation frame, the clean-up budget, the structural tolerance and the break tolerance. The defaults suit most mechanisms; raise the iterations if long chains lag behind the pointer.

    View and workspace

    View F fit, Shift+R rotate

    Fit centres the whole mechanism on screen. The rotate-view dial turns the whole picture (snapping to 5°, Shift for any angle); type an exact angle under its centre, double-click the centre for 0°. The rotation is purely visual: the document, its axes, grid and snapping all stay in world axes.

    Grid and snap

    The grid button shows or hides the grid; the snap button makes placement and dragging land on grid points. The spacing is chosen from the list next to them, in working units; type your own value to add it to the list.

    Object snap

    With object snap on, a dragged body is carried by the corner, edge midpoint, edge or centre nearest to where you grabbed it (highlighted), and that feature snaps onto the same features of other bodies, rails and guidelines.

    Light and dark theme

    Toggles the interface between the dark and light palettes. The choice is remembered; this manual follows it.

    Files

    Saving and loading Ctrl+S, Ctrl+O

    Documents are JSON files. In Chromium browsers Ctrl+S saves in place: the first save asks where, later saves overwrite silently, and the title shows a dot while the document has unsaved changes. Ctrl+Shift+S saves as a new file. Elsewhere, saving downloads a timestamped copy. Load with Ctrl+O or drop a file onto the canvas; DXF files are imported as bodies. The trash button clears the whole document (or, inside a component, just that definition).

    Export cut files

    Writes a DXF (for CAM: routers, plasma) or an SVG in millimetres (for laser software) of the selected bodies, or all bodies when nothing is selected. Optionally adds a drill hole at every joint, on its own layer.

    Auto-backup

    Periodically writes timestamped copies of the mechanism into a folder you choose, a set time after the first change since the last backup or save, and prunes the folder to the last few copies. The panel shows the countdown to the next backup. The clock button turns amber when backups are paused waiting for folder access.

    On the canvas

    Body

    A rigid part of the mechanism: a filled outline in its own colour. Bodies never bend or stretch; they move as a whole, carried by their joints. Click one to select it and reveal its shape handles; drag it to move it (in Simulate mode, dragging drives the mechanism instead). Drawn with the Body tool, recoloured with the colour swatch.

    Grounded body

    A body fixed entirely in the world: it cannot move or turn in simulation, so everything attached to it works against a fixed frame. Made with the Ground tool on the body itself; click it with the tool again to release it.

    Group

    Several bodies that select and move as one and behave as a single rigid body in simulation. Clicking any member selects the whole group. Made and dissolved with Ctrl+G.

    Hole

    A cut-out in a body. Holes have the same corner handles and rounding as the outline and are exported with it as inner contours. Round holes can carry a diameter dimension and be repeated by a pattern. Cut with the Hole tool.

    Shape handles

    When a body is selected, small squares mark its control corners and small circles sit on each rounded corner. Drag a square to move the corner (the outline follows), drag a circle along the corner to change its radius, Ctrl-drag a circle to round every corner alike. Double-click an edge to add a corner, double-click a corner to remove it. The same handles appear on holes.

    Joint

    A point attached to a body. On its own it is just a marked point that moves with the body; it becomes a hinge, an anchor or a slider once a constraint uses it. Its fill tells its role: plain, blue when pinned, yellow when grounded, green when it rides a rail. Placed with the Joint tool; delete it and its constraints go with it.

    Free joint

    A joint that belongs to no body. Use one as a fixed anchor by grounding it, as an end of a fixed track, or as a corner to build a body around with the Body tool.

    Pin

    Two joints held at the same point while their bodies turn freely about it: the hinge of a linkage, drawn as a blue joint. Made with Connect. Click it with the Weld tool to lock the rotation too.

    Weld

    A pin that also locks the two bodies' relative angle, so they move as one rigid piece while staying separate parts. Made with the Weld tool; clicking a weld with it turns it back into a pin.

    Ground

    A joint fixed to a point in the world, drawn yellow with the ground symbol beneath. The body may still turn about it. Grounds are absolute for the solver: when the rest of the mechanism cannot reach them the assembly is reported as impossible. Made with the Ground tool.

    Rail

    A track between two joints, drawn as a double-headed arrow. When both end joints sit on one body the rail moves with it; when they are free joints it is fixed in the world. Joints placed on it become riders. Drawn with the Rail tool or, together with its carriage, by the Slider tool.

    Rider

    A joint constrained to stay on a rail while its body still turns: a pin in a slot. Drawn green. Click it with the Slider tool to lock its body's orientation, which turns it into a slider.

    Slider

    A rider whose body is also locked to the rail's direction, so the body translates without rotating: a prismatic joint. Built in two clicks with the Slider tool; click it with the tool again to unlock the rotation. A slider can be driven by a linear actuator.

    Linear actuator

    A self-driving rail: in animation its carriage travels back and forth between the rail's ends at the configured speed and profile. Select it to edit them in the toolbar. Made with the Linear actuator tool; run it with play.

    Motor

    A pivot and a crank pin on one body: in animation the crank pin orbits the pivot at the configured angular speed, turning the body. Select either joint to edit the speed. Made with the Motor tool.

    Guideline

    An infinite construction line with a small handle at each defining point. It takes part in snapping and constraints but is not part of the mechanism and never exports. Drag a handle to re-aim it; select and delete it like anything else. Placed with the Guideline tool.

    Dimension

    A measurement between two references with its value on a label. A driven dimension just reports; double-click the label and type a value to make it driving, and the geometry moves to match. Drag the label to reposition it. In Simulate mode dimensions are live read-outs. See Dimensions and the Measure tool.

    Context dimension

    A temporary, muted dimension between the definition you are editing and the faded context ghost around it. It is never saved and lives only while this definition is open. Double-click its value and type a number to move the live side to that distance once.

    Constraint badge

    The small glyph marking a sketch constraint on an edge, rail or point pair. Each kind has its own topic: coincident, horizontal, vertical, parallel, perpendicular, equal. Click a badge to select it, Delete to remove the constraint. The eye button in the constraint group hides all badges. See Sketch constraints.

    Pattern

    A hole or joint repeated along one or two directions, or around a centre. Its dotted axis line, handles and labels are drawn beside the members: drag a handle to re-aim it, click a label to edit count, spacing or angle. Members that do not fit are flagged. Made with the linear and circular pattern tools.

    Component instance

    A placed copy of a component definition. Its bodies and joints select as one unit, and every instance follows its definition. Double-click it to edit the definition; Ctrl+double-click to edit it in the context of the surrounding assembly. See Components.

    Context ghost

    The faded drawing of the enclosing assembly shown while a component definition is edited in context. It is inert: it cannot be selected, dragged or constrained, but its corners, midpoints, edges and joints are object-snap targets and the Measure tool can place context dimensions onto it. The breadcrumb eyes choose how many enclosing levels are shown. See Editing a definition in context.

    Getting help

    Click the help button (or press ?) to open this manual beside the canvas. While it is open the pointer is a question mark and every control you click shows its topic here instead of acting; clicking the canvas shows the topic for the armed tool or the current mode. Close it with the help button, ?, Esc or the ✕ to get back to work. F1 opens the manual at the table of contents. Drag the drawer's left edge to resize it; the arrow button opens the manual in its own tab.
