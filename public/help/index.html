Getting started

Disjointed manual

Disjointed is a 2D planar mechanism simulator. You draw rigid bodies, place joints on them, couple the joints with pins, grounds, rails and sliders, then switch to Simulate mode and drag anything to see the whole linkage move. In Draw mode the drawing behaves like a CAD sketch: constraints and driving dimensions hold while you edit.

This manual opens beside the canvas. In the app, click the help button (or press ?) to open it; while it is open, click any control — or anything drawn on the canvas — to jump to its topic. F1 brings you back to this table of contents.

    Tutorial: your first mechanism

    A four-bar linkage in five steps. Every step uses a single tool; each tool disarms itself after one use.

    1. Draw three bars. Press B (Polyline, Body role) and click four corners for a long, thin rectangle; click the first corner again or press Enter to close it. Repeat for two more bars. Select a bar and press ] a few times to round its ends.
    2. Add joints. Press J (Joint) and click near each end of every bar. A joint belongs to the body it is placed on.
    3. Pin the bars together. Press Ctrl+Shift+C (Connect), click a joint on one bar, then a joint on the next. The second joint snaps onto the first. Do this twice to make a chain of three bars.
    4. Ground the two free ends. Press G (Ground) and click the joint at each end of the chain. A grounded joint stays where it is but still turns.
    5. Simulate. Press Tab to switch to Simulate mode and drag the middle bar. The whole linkage follows.

    Right-drag pans the view, the mouse wheel zooms, Shift+F fits the mechanism to the screen. The line along the bottom of the window always says what the next click will do.

    The canvas

    The canvas is the drawing. What a click does depends on the mode and on the armed tool; with no tool armed, clicking selects and dragging moves. The status bar along the bottom of the window spells out what the armed tool expects, or what you can do with the current selection (hover it to read the whole line when it is a long one).

    • Pan: drag with the right mouse button.
    • Zoom: mouse wheel, about the pointer.
    • Fit: Shift+F, or the fit button in the View group.
    • Cancel: Esc drops a half-drawn shape, disarms the tool, or clears the selection.

    When something can't be done — an invalid pick, a rejected cut, a constraint that can't hold — a toast slides in under the toolbar and says why; click it to dismiss it sooner.

    Draw and Simulate modes Tab toggles

    The big button in the Mode group switches between building the mechanism and running it. It shows the mode it switches to — the play triangle while you draw, the pencil while you simulate — and the caption under it names the mode you are in. Entering Simulate settles every constraint once; leaving it puts every body back exactly where you drew it. Groups that belong to the other mode leave the toolbar while you are not in it.

    Draw mode

    Build the mechanism: draw bodies, place joints, add constraints and dimensions, arrange and edit everything. Bodies move freely when dragged, and the sketch solver keeps any dimensions and constraints you added satisfied while you edit. Hold Shift while dragging to move a body rigidly with its linked neighbours instead — the way it would move in simulation, with everything else frozen.

    Simulate mode

    Drag any joint, or any point on a body, and the constraint solver propagates the motion through everything pinned, grounded or sliding with it. Nothing you do here changes the drawing: switching back to Draw mode restores the drawn layout. Press Space or the play button to run actuators and motors. Reference geometry and constraint badges are hidden here; dimensions stay and read live.

    Toolbar and drawing tools

    The toolbar

    Controls are gathered into named groups — File, Mode, Role, Shapes, Colour, Pattern, Boolean, Mating, Actuators, Transform, Components, Constraints, Grid, Measure, Snapping and View, plus Animation in Simulate mode. Each group is two rows tall and reads down, then across, with its name on the caption strip underneath. Drag a group by its caption to move it along the toolbar: the others slide into the order they will have when you let go, and the arrangement is remembered. Double-click a caption to restore the default order. On a narrow window the toolbar wraps whole groups onto a second row.

    A group whose drawing can be hidden carries a small eye on its caption — Grid, Constraints (the badges) and Measure (the dimensions). A struck-through eye means that group's drawing is hidden, never that it stopped working. Fields that belong to a selection or an armed tool — text height, actuator and motor speed, the solver knobs — appear in a strip to the right of the groups, so nothing shifts when a selection comes or goes; the ? help button sits at the far right.

    Every drawing tool is one-shot: arm it with its button or key, use it once, and you are back in select mode (Esc disarms it early). Every tooltip ends with the control's shortcut, read from the keymap rather than written into the button — a control with no key shows none, and one you rebind says so straight away. The status bar along the bottom says what the armed tool wants you to click next.

    Shape roles: Body, Cut, Reference 1 2 3

    The shape tools (polyline, rectangle, circle, regular polygon, slot) draw plain geometry; the role switch decides what a finished shape becomes. Body: a new rigid body. Cut: the shape is subtracted from the body under it — a hole when it lies inside, a notch when it crosses the outline (a cut that would sever a body is refused; use Split). Reference: construction geometry — dash-dot, snappable and measurable, never simulated or exported. Line, arc and text are always reference geometry.

    The role is sticky, so a row of holes is drawn without touching it again. The preview shows the role as you draw (a tint for a body, hatching plus the highlighted target for a cut, dash-dot for a reference) and a small badge beside the cursor names it. To flip one shape between Body and Cut, hold Ctrl on its first click. A cut takes the selected body as its target when one is selected (select the plate, then cut), otherwise the topmost body under the first click, otherwise the body the finished shape lies in — so bodies may overlap freely and you still choose what gets cut.

    Polyline B body · Ctrl+U cut

    Click each corner, then click the first corner again, double-click or press Enter to close. Clicks that land on existing joints, corners, edge midpoints or reference points are remembered and turned into coincident constraints, so a body drawn between two joints stays attached to them; near-horizontal / vertical edges are straightened and get the matching constraint. B arms it in the Body role, Ctrl+U in the Cut role; in the Reference role a closed polyline is a reference polygon and two points make a segment.

    In the Body role, start on existing free joints instead and the body is built around them: the joints become its joints, expanded outward by a margin you set with the last click. Corners can be rounded afterwards with [ and ], or by dragging a corner's fillet handle; Ctrl-drag rounds every corner at once.

    Rectangle Shift+B

    Click one corner, then the opposite corner — or press and drag. Hold Shift for a square, Alt to draw from the centre. The four edges get horizontal / vertical constraints, so the rectangle stays one under later edits.

    Circle C

    Click the centre, then a point on the rim — or press and drag the radius out. As a body it is a disk (a one-point rounded outline: move it by its centre node, resize it by its rim handle); as a cut, a round hole that can carry a diameter dimension and be repeated by a pattern; as a reference, a circle whose centre snaps and measures. Every kind of circle can be made tangent to a line and equal in radius to another.

    Regular polygon Shift+P

    Click the centre, then one corner — or press and drag. The side count shows beside the cursor while the tool is armed; ↑ / ↓ change it. Once the polygon exists it stays regular: drag a corner to grow or spin it about its centre; an "n sides" tag above the selected polygon shows the count — double-click it to type a new one, or press ↑ / ↓. Nodes can't be added or removed by double-click (change the count instead). Its centre is drawn as a small crosshair and is a point for coincident constraints, dimensions and object snap.

    In the sketch a regular polygon is a rigid shape. Its size is set by a size dimension — an edge, a chord between any two corners, centre to corner, centre to edge, across flats between opposite edges, or the horizontal / vertical span between two corners (what a label placed between the corners gives you) — and it scales to the value, keeping its rotation. Its rotation is set by drags and by line constraints (Horizontal, Vertical, Parallel or Perpendicular on an edge turn it in one step). Every other dimension or constraint moves or turns it whole, and a corner drag then only does what the constraints leave free: with the size driven it only turns, with the rotation locked it only resizes, with both it moves the whole polygon. A size the polygon can't reach, or a second size that disagrees with the first, is refused with the conflicting dimension flashed. Split or combine turns it into a free polygon. As a cut it makes a regular hole (a nut trap) with the same behaviour.

    Slot Shift+S

    Click where the slot starts and where it ends — or press and drag — then move out to set its width and click. A slot is a two-point rounded outline (a capsule): as a body a link with rounded ends, as a cut a slotted hole. In the Reference role the two clicks make the axis segment.

    Line L

    A finite reference segment: click two points, or press and drag. Points that land on joints, corners, edge midpoints or other reference points are held there by a coincident constraint. Its endpoints snap and measure, its midpoint is a snap and alignment target, and the segment takes horizontal / vertical / parallel / perpendicular / equal constraints and length dimensions like a body edge. Snapping and picking reach only as far as the segment itself. It replaces the infinite guideline of earlier versions: draw it as long as you need it.

    Arc A

    A reference arc: click its start and end points, then a point it should pass through. Its three points are handles and references; placements snap onto the arc itself, and a line can be made tangent to it. Its radius follows its three points, so Equal can copy it but never set it.

    Text Shift+T

    A label. Click where it goes, type, press Enter. Clicked on a body, the label is anchored to that body and rides with it; on empty space it stays where it is. The height (in working units) is set in the field that appears in the toolbar's properties strip while the tool is armed. Double-click a label to edit it, drag it to move it, select and Delete to remove it. Labels are drawing aids: never simulated or exported.

    Split X

    Cut a body in two. Click a point on its outline, route the cut through the inside with more clicks, and click the outline again to finish. Existing corners keep their rounding, the cut corners start sharp; holes stay whole on their side (a cut through a hole is refused), and joints stay with whichever half they land in. The Boolean operations are the other direction: they build one body out of several.

    Combine N

    The three Boolean operations work on a multi-selection — Ctrl+click the bodies, or box-select them — and share one convention: the first selected body survives (the one you Ctrl+clicked first, or the bottom-most in a box selection), keeping its colour, joints, grounding, group and stacking position, and the others are consumed. Corners that survive keep their rounding, new corners are sharp, holes carry over (a round hole stays a true disk), and dimensions on corners that survive follow them. Bodies built from joints are turned into an editable outline first.

    Combine merges the selected bodies into one — the union of their shapes. They must overlap or share an edge (bodies that only touch at a corner are refused). Pins between the combined bodies disappear, since they would be inside one body; motors and rails carry over and groups merge. A region the union closes off becomes a new hole.

    Subtract

    Cut the other selected bodies out of the first selected one. A cutter lying entirely inside becomes a hole of the cutter's own shape — a disk body punches a true round hole, a rounded rectangle a rounded one — and a cutter crossing the outline leaves a notch. The cutters are consumed, and their joints and constraints go with them; copy a cutter first if you want to keep it. Refused, with a message, when a cutter doesn't overlap the first body (touching along an edge doesn't count), or when the cut would remove the body, split it into several pieces (use Split) or leave it touching itself at a point.

    Intersect

    Keep only the overlap of the selected bodies: the first selected survives, reshaped to the region common to all of them, and the others are consumed. Holes inside the overlap stay. Refused when a body doesn't overlap the first, when the bodies share no area, or when the overlap comes in several separate pieces.

    Joint J

    Add a joint point. Click on a body to attach the joint to it; joints on the same body move rigidly together. Click on empty space for a free joint, which can be grounded as an anchor or used to build a body around later. With object snap on, the joint lands on the nearest corner, edge midpoint, centre, hole centre or joint in range. A joint placed on a rail becomes a rider on that rail. An attached joint always lands — and stays — inside its body.

    Connect (pin) Ctrl+Shift+C

    Pin two joints together so they share a position but rotate freely. Click the first joint, then the second; the second moves onto the first. Pins are the hinges of a linkage. Click a rail instead of a second joint to attach the joint to it as a rider.

    Weld W

    Lock two overlapping bodies rigidly together at a point, with no relative rotation. Click where the bodies overlap. Click an existing pinned joint to toggle it between a pin and a weld.

    Ground G

    Fix a joint in the world: it keeps its position but still turns. Click a body or a group instead to fix it entirely. Click a grounded element again to release it. Grounds are sacred to the solver: they never move, so an assembly that cannot reach them is reported as impossible.

    Rail no default key

    Draw a track between two joints. Two joints on the same body make a moving rail that travels with that body; two free joints make a fixed track in the world. Joints placed on a rail ride along it like a pin in a slot. Rails draw as double-headed arrows. Give the tool a key through the Shortcuts panel if you use it often.

    Slider S

    Build a complete prismatic joint in two clicks. Click the body that should slide where its travel starts, then click where the travel ends: the body now slides along the arrow without rotating. If the second click lands on another body, the track rides that body; otherwise it is fixed in the world.

    Click an existing rail to add a slider on it, or click a rider to toggle its rotation lock (a locked rider is a slider; an unlocked one is a pin in a slot).

    Linear pattern I

    Repeat a hole or a joint along one or two directions. Click the hole (inside its cut-out) or the joint, then where the next instance goes: the row appears and its ×count label opens, so type the count and press Enter. The tool stays armed for an optional second direction — click where the first instance of the other direction goes to make a grid — or press Enter / Esc to keep a single row. A direction within ~5° of horizontal / vertical gets the matching constraint, like a body edge.

    Several features at once: Ctrl+click several holes and joints of one body (Ctrl+click a picked one to drop it; the layout preview shows once you release Ctrl). They repeat as a group, so every instance reproduces their relative placement, and Ctrl+click still adds or removes seeds after the row exists. The first pick is the anchor the dotted line starts from.

    Circular pattern Q

    Repeat a hole or a joint — or, with Ctrl+click, several features of one body — around a centre. Pick the seed(s), then click the centre of rotation (it snaps to joints, hole centres, corners and the grid): six instances spread evenly and the count label opens for typing. The angle between instances and whether the members turn with the arc are edited on the canvas labels — see Pattern.

    Constraints and dimensions

    Sketch constraints

    Constraints are geometric rules between points, lines and circles that the sketch solver keeps satisfied in Draw mode: Coincident, Equal, Horizontal, Vertical, Parallel, Perpendicular, Tangential, Symmetrical and Fixed. The geometry moves to satisfy a constraint the moment you place it, and it keeps holding through every later edit — drags included. Each constraint draws a small violet badge beside its element (◎ H V ∥ ⊥ =, a padlock for Fixed, a dashed mirror with a dot each side for Symmetrical, a circle touching a line for Tangential), faded until you hover the element. Hovering a badge highlights the elements it constrains; click it to select it, Delete to remove the constraint. The eye on the group's caption shows or hides all badges (constraints keep working while hidden).

    A refusal is always explained. A constraint that can't be satisfied is rejected — nothing moves, a toast says why, and the items it collided with flash red for a few seconds so you can find them: "Coincident can't be applied: it conflicts with the Horizontal constraint and the 40 mm dimension (flashing red)." A pick the tool can't use is explained the same way rather than ignored ("Parallel needs two lines…", "That element is already fixed."), and so is a dimension value the sketch can't reach.

    Who moves. The more mobile side gives way: reference geometry is construction and moves before body corners and joints; the element you are dragging never gets tugged back; a component instance's shape belongs to its definition and never changes (the constraint moves the whole instance instead); a point locked with Fixed never moves at all. The common cases need no tool: see implicit constraints while dragging, and the auto-constraints applied while you draw (near-level edges, a corner placed on a joint).

    Implicit constraints while dragging

    While dragging a body, joint, corner node or hole centre, hold the dragged point over another element for about half a second — a corner, hole centre, joint, reference point, the midpoint of an edge / rail / reference segment, or an edge, rail or reference segment — and it becomes an alignment candidate, lit violet. A point under the cursor always wins over the line it lies on, so holding the middle of an edge arms its midpoint and holding elsewhere along it arms the edge. Now move on: when the dragged point lines up horizontally or vertically with a candidate point, lands on a candidate point, or lands on a candidate line, a dotted violet line with the constraint's badge (H, V or ◎) previews the alignment. Release while it shows and the geometry is nudged exactly into alignment and the constraint is created — the same one the tools make, badge and all. Dropping a point right on a candidate point makes a coincident.

    Two candidates stay armed at once, so one drag can land vertical to one reference and horizontal to another, or on a reference line and level with a corner: each shows its own dotted line and badge, and the release places both and lands the geometry exactly on the intersection. Hovering a third element drops the oldest candidate; Esc drops the most recent one while the drag goes on. Two alignments that pull the same way (two horizontals, say) can't both be exact, so only the more recently armed one previews; a point-on-point coincident uses up both directions and always previews alone. A placement that can't be satisfied loses only that one constraint, with a message; anything else previewed is still placed.

    The switch at the left of the Constraints group — a tall pill with a slider, deliberately unlike the square tools beside it — turns the whole thing off and on. Like the snap toggles it starts on and is not remembered between sessions. The auto-constraints applied while you draw are a separate thing and stay on.

    Coincident O

    What it does: two points are held at the same place, or a point is held somewhere on a line (a body edge, a rail or a reference segment), while everything else stays free. This is how a body corner stays attached to a joint, or a joint stays on an edge, as you edit around it. Shapes drawn onto existing points get coincident constraints automatically, and a drag dropped on an armed point gets one too.

    To add one: click two points (joints, body corners, a regular polygon's centre, reference points), or a point and a line in either order. Its badge is a small circled dot at the shared point.

    Horizontal H

    What it does: keeps a body edge, rail or reference segment level, or two points at the same height, in world axes. The sketch solver re-levels it after every edit, so you can drag other corners without the line tilting. Dragging into a level alignment also offers it as an implicit constraint.

    To add one: click a line, or two points. Its badge is an H glyph on the line.

    Vertical V

    What it does: keeps a body edge, rail or reference segment upright, or two points on the same vertical line, in world axes. Like Horizontal, it holds through every later edit and is offered implicitly while dragging.

    To add one: click a line, or two points. Its badge is a V glyph on the line.

    Parallel P

    What it does: keeps two lines (body edges, rails or reference segments, on the same body or different ones) at the same angle without fixing that angle: turn one and the other follows. A linear pattern's dotted direction line counts as a line too.

    To add one: click the two lines. A matching pair of badges marks both.

    Perpendicular T

    What it does: keeps two lines (body edges, rails or reference segments) at a right angle to each other, whatever their absolute orientation. Useful for square corners that must stay square while the body is reshaped.

    To add one: click the two lines. A matching pair of badges marks both.

    Equal E

    What it does: keeps two lines the same length — stretch one and the other stretches with it — or two radii the same: a disk body's rim, a round hole, a rounded corner's arc, a reference circle or a reference arc, in any mix. Combine it with a driving dimension on one of them to size both. Equals chain: A = B and B = C keep all three alike.

    To add one: click two lines (body edges, rails or reference segments), or two circles / rounded corners — the second takes the first one's value. A line and a circle together are refused, and a sharp corner has no arc to click (round it a little first). One = badge sits at each element; on radii it turns red while they differ.

    Who follows, for radii: a driving diameter or radius dimension anywhere in the chain wins (a second dimension that disagrees is rejected, both flashing); a reference arc and component geometry can only be copied, never resized; otherwise the element you resize directly — rim or radius handle, [ / ], a reference circle's handle — is the reference, and the partners follow (their size dimensions go back to driven, like the resized outline's own). A disk that follows keeps its tangent lines on the new rim.

    Fixed F

    What it does: locks one element where it is. A point — a joint, a body corner, a regular polygon’s centre, a reference point — keeps its exact position: nothing moves it again, not another constraint, not a dimension, not even dragging it. A line — a body edge, a rail, a reference segment — is pinned as a whole, angle and position: its two ends stay free, but only to slide along that line and stretch it. The line itself can never turn or shift.

    To add one: click the point or the line. One click is the whole gesture, and its badge is a small padlock on the locked element — the same icon as the toolbar button.

    Use it to nail a mechanism down while you sketch the rest against it: everything constrained to a locked element yields to it instead of dragging it along. Two locks that contradict each other (a coincident between two locked points, a parallel between two lines locked on different tracks) are refused, and the conflicting items flash red. Mirroring a locked body re-anchors its locks to the mirrored geometry — the lock holds it there rather than fighting a move you asked for. A locked element looks like any other apart from its badge, so when a body deforms around a point under a drag, look for the padlock.

    Symmetrical Y

    What it does: makes two elements mirror images of each other across a line. Two points — joints, body corners, a regular polygon’s centre, reference points — end up at the same perpendicular distance from the mirror line, on opposite sides and on one perpendicular to it. Two lines — body edges, rails, reference segments — mirror as whole lines, angle and offset; their endpoints stay free, so the two need not be the same length or line up end to end.

    To add one: three clicks — the two points (or the two lines), then the mirror line — a body edge, a rail or a reference segment; the status bar says which click comes next. Its badge is a small dashed mirror with a dot each side, the same picture as the toolbar button, on both elements and on the mirror line; hovering one links the pair.

    Who moves follows the usual rule: the more mobile side gives way. A free reference line used as the mirror is itself re-placed onto the pair’s bisector by its first symmetry; once it carries two symmetries, is snapped to geometry or is locked with Fixed, it stays and the pairs come to it. Dragging one of a symmetric pair swings the other to keep the mirror image.

    Tangential Z

    What it does: holds a line tangent to a circle or an arc — the circle’s centre sits exactly one radius off the line, on the side it already is, so the line just touches the rim. The circle can be a disk body, a round hole, a reference circle or a reference arc; the line a body edge, a rail or a reference segment.

    To add one: click the circle (its rim) and the line, in either order; while the tool is armed a circle under the cursor highlights as its whole rim, and the status bar says which pick is still missing. Its badge — a small circle touching a line, the same picture as the toolbar button — sits at the contact point; hovering it highlights the rim and the line.

    The radius never changes for a tangent: a disk is sized by its diameter dimension or its rim handle, and a reference arc moves as a rigid piece. Who moves follows the usual rule — the more mobile side gives way: a free reference circle comes to a body edge, a disk comes to an edge locked with Fixed, a locked disk pushes the edge, and two free bodies meet halfway. Dragging the disk slides it along the line; driving its diameter moves the line out to the new rim; a circle dragged through its line re-attaches on the far side.

    To blend a line smoothly into an arc, add the tangent and then a Coincident between the arc’s end and the line’s end: the line then touches the arc exactly at the shared end, and dragging that end swings the line with it.

    Measure D

    Place a dimension: click two references — points (joints, body corners, hole corners, reference points, a regular polygon's centre, or any point inside a body) or lines (body and hole edges, rails, reference segments, a pattern's direction) — then click where the value should sit. Or click one line and then click on nothing: that places the line's own length, a plain dimension between its two ends (hovering empty space previews it; hovering another reference highlights it, and clicking it makes the usual line-to-point or line-to-line dimension instead). Click the rim of a round hole or disk as the first pick for a diameter dimension (⌀), or a rounded corner's arc for a radius dimension (R) — neither needs a second reference.

    What a pair measures follows from what you picked: two points give a horizontal, vertical or direct distance depending on where you place the value (above or below the pair → horizontal, beside it → vertical, in the diagonal zones → direct); a point and a line, the perpendicular distance; two lines, their distance while parallel and their angle otherwise. Dimensions placed in Simulate mode are live read-outs that follow the motion; each mode keeps its own set.

    Dimensions

    A dimension starts driven: it reports the current value, in parentheses. Double-click its label and type a number to make it driving: the geometry moves to match and stays there while you edit elsewhere. Clear the value to make it driven again. A value the sketch can't reach is refused, with a toast naming what held the geometry and a red flash on it. The eye on the Measure group's caption hides all dimensions, in both modes.

    A point–point dimension's direction is fixed when it is placed. The pill leads with a small arrow saying which — horizontal, vertical, or a rising diagonal for a direct distance — and dragging the pill afterwards only moves the pill. Click the arrow to change the direction: each click cycles horizontal → vertical → direct, and a driving dimension keeps its value and re-solves along the new direction (or the change is refused with the usual flash). A pill dragged past the end of its dimension line gets a dashed leader back to it, so the value still reads as belonging to it. Diameter, radius, point–line, line–line and angle dimensions carry no arrow.

    What a driving dimension moves. The first driving dimension on an otherwise-unconstrained body scales the whole body uniformly — unless an end of it is on a hole: a hole's own width, diameter or distance from a corner changes just that hole, and the outline stays. Further dimensions move only the involved nodes while every constraint and driving dimension holds, and a body moved by a dimension moves as a whole, holes and joints riding along. A driving diameter or radius sets that disk or corner directly (every corner of the outline when they are uniform), and every radius made Equal to it. A dimension on a pattern's seed drives it like any hole and every instance copies the result; only a distance between two instances is refused — that is the pattern's spacing, edited on its label.

    On components a dimension never changes a shape: between two instances it moves one of them rigidly (a grounded one stays put); between two mobile parts of one component it re-poses the internal mechanism; internal to one rigid piece it is rejected, since that is dimensioned inside the definition.

    Editing

    Selecting and editing

    • Select by clicking; Ctrl+click adds to the selection; drag a box from empty space to select everything inside.
    • Move by dragging a selected body or joint. Snapping follows the grid, reference geometry and object snap; hold Shift to move rigidly with the linked neighbours instead.
    • Edit a shape: a selected body shows its corner handles; drag them, double-click an edge to add a corner, double-click a corner to remove it (a regular polygon keeps its shape — change its side count instead).
    • Feature selection: with one body selected, Shift+drag a box to select some of its corners, holes and joints and move, delete or copy them together.
    • Delete with Delete or Backspace; undo with Ctrl+Z, redo with Ctrl+Y.
    • Copy and paste with Ctrl+C / Ctrl+V: a body travels with its joints, internal constraints and dimensions; the paste lands at the pointer. A feature selection pastes its holes and joints into the selected body.
    • Group with Ctrl+G so several bodies select and move as one; press it again on a group to dissolve it. Double-click a member to edit the parts inside.

    Editing inside a group

    A group is one object — until you double-click a member. That opens the group for editing from the inside, the way a component definition opens: its bodies and joints select, drag and reshape one at a time, while everything outside drops to 20% strength and goes inert — it can't be selected or dragged until you leave, but it is still a snap and constraint target, so you can align and dimension the group's parts against the rest of the drawing. Dimensions, constraint badges and patterns that touch the group stay at full strength and stay editable; those that don't fade with the surroundings. The grid never fades: it is the paper, not the drawing.

    A breadcrumb bar at the top left names what you are inside (Assembly ▸ Group (2 parts)). Leave with Esc (twice if something is selected), a double-click on empty space, or a click on a crumb; the group is selected whole again. It is a Draw-mode view of your own document: nothing is saved, nothing lands in undo, and it ends by itself if the group is dissolved or undone away. Note that Ctrl+G inside a group dissolves it — the selection is the group.

    Rotate R

    Turn the selected body about its centroid, or grab one of its nodes to turn it about that node. The angle snaps to multiples of 45° near them. With several bodies selected the whole set turns together. Rotate is a mode rather than a one-shot tool; press Esc or pick another tool to leave it.

    Mirror

    Flip the selection left-right or top-bottom in place, about the selection's own centre. Joints, constraints and dimensions inside the selection mirror with it; a Fixed lock re-anchors to the mirrored geometry. A component instance mirrors into its definition's mirror image and stays linked to it.

    Stacking order PageUp / PageDown

    Bring the selected body in front of everything else, or send it behind. Purely visual — but clicks always pick the topmost body, so this also decides what a click lands on. The order is saved with the file.

    Body colour

    The swatch in the Colour group sets the active colour. With nothing selected it is the colour new bodies get; with a body selected it shows that body's colour and editing it recolours the body. With a multi-selection or a group selected it recolours every body in it at once (showing the first member's colour when they differ). Component instances are left out — an instance takes its colour from its definition, so open the definition to recolour it; a toast says when some were skipped.

    Working units

    One world unit is one of the chosen unit (millimetres by default), set from the dropdown in the Grid group. Changing the unit moves nothing; it only changes how the grid, dimensions, imports and exports interpret the numbers. The choice is saved with the file.

    Components

    Components

    A component packs a selection into a reusable definition and replaces it with an instance. Every instance follows its definition: edit one and all of them change. With an instance selected, the button forks it into a new independent component; with nothing selected it creates an empty component and opens it for editing. Instances can be mirrored and rotated like bodies. Their shapes are design-locked: constraints and dimensions on an instance move it as a whole (or pose its internal mechanism) rather than reshaping it.

    Component browser

    Lists every definition in the document. From here you insert an instance (click the canvas to place it), rename, edit or delete a definition, and drag rows to reorder the list. Hovering a row highlights its instances on the canvas; hovering an instance highlights its row.

    Editing a definition in context

    Double-click an instance to open its definition; all normal tools work inside. The breadcrumb bar at the top left shows where you are — definitions can nest, and an open group adds a crumb of its own — and clicking a crumb takes you back out (so does Esc with nothing selected). Ctrl+double-click opens the definition with the whole enclosing assembly drawn faded around it: this context ghost is inert, but you can snap to it and place temporary dimensions onto it to position the definition against its surroundings. The eye on each breadcrumb chooses how many enclosing levels are shown.

    Simulation

    Running the animation Space

    In Simulate mode the play button drives every linear actuator and motor at once. Pausing and playing resumes smoothly from the current pose. The warning-triangle button next to it pauses the animation automatically when the assembly becomes impossible.

    Linear actuator Shift+A

    Click a slider or rail to make it self-driving: its carriage travels back and forth between the rail's ends during animation. Select it to set the speed and the motion profile (triangle for constant speed, sine for a smooth ease at the ends) in the toolbar's properties strip.

    Motor Shift+M

    Pick a pivot joint and then a crank pin on the same body: in animation the crank pin orbits the pivot at the configured angular speed. Select the motor to set its speed.

    Impossible assemblies

    When the constraints cannot all be satisfied at once (a link too short to reach its ground, for example), the solver keeps the grounds fixed, isolates the pins and sliders it cannot close, and still solves the rest. The breaks are drawn as red dotted lines and a banner appears at the top of the canvas.

    Solver tuning

    The four controls in the properties strip (Simulate mode) adjust how hard the simulation works each frame: iterations per animation frame, the clean-up budget, the structural tolerance and the break tolerance. The defaults suit most mechanisms; raise the iterations if long chains lag behind the pointer.

    View and workspace

    View Shift+F fit, Ctrl+R rotate

    Fit centres the whole mechanism on screen. The rotate-view dial turns the whole picture (snapping to 5°, Shift for any angle); type an exact angle under its centre, double-click the centre or press 0 for upright. The rotation is purely visual: the document, its axes, grid and snapping all stay in world axes, and labels stay upright.

    Grid

    The eye on the group's caption shows or hides the grid; snapping to it is the Snap button in the Snapping group, and the two are independent (you can snap to a hidden grid). The spacing is chosen from the list, in working units; type your own value in the Custom field to add it to the list. The units dropdown beside it declares what one world unit means.

    The grid's look is yours: the style picker shows a sample of the line itself — solid, dashed or dotted lines, or points (a dot at each intersection, the least obtrusive) — and the colour swatch tints it. The colour is kept per theme, since a tone that reads against the dark background disappears against the light one; the swatch edits the theme you are in, and right-click it to return that theme to its default tone. Both settings are remembered. Lines and dots keep the same on-screen weight at any zoom; the points grid stops drawing when it would merge into a wash.

    Snapping

    Snap makes placement and dragging land on grid points — or, in preference to the grid, on nearby reference geometry (projected onto a reference edge within its span, onto a rim, or onto the intersection of two reference edges).

    Object snap makes objects snap to each other. A dragged body is carried by the feature nearest to where you grabbed it — a corner, then an edge midpoint, then an edge, else its centre (highlighted, and previewed as you hover) — and that feature snaps onto the same features of other bodies, joints, rails and reference geometry: corners, the midpoints of edges, rails and reference segments, centres, edges. An edge snaps onto parallel edges only, sliding until the two are flush. When a point target and a line are both in range the point wins, so sliding along an edge jumps onto its midpoint as you pass it. Object snap also applies to reshaping (a dragged corner node or hole centre snaps onto the other features) and to placement (a new joint or hole centre lands on the nearest corner, midpoint, centre or joint in range).

    Light and dark theme

    Toggles the interface between the dark and light palettes. The choice is remembered; this manual follows it, and so does the grid colour you set for each theme.

    Files

    Saving and loading Ctrl+S, Ctrl+O

    Documents are JSON files. In Chromium browsers Ctrl+S saves in place: the first save asks where, later saves overwrite silently, and the title shows a dot while the document has unsaved changes. Ctrl+Shift+S saves as a new file. Elsewhere, saving downloads a timestamped copy. Load with Ctrl+O or drop a file onto the canvas; DXF files are imported as bodies, at true scale, with their fillets and circles editable. The trash button clears the whole document (or, inside a component, just that definition). Your work is also auto-saved in the browser and restored when you return.

    Export cut files

    Writes a DXF (for CAM: routers, plasma) or an SVG in millimetres (for laser software) of the selected bodies, or all bodies when nothing is selected. Rounded corners, disks and rounded hulls export as exact arcs, every hole as its own loop. Optionally adds a drill hole at every joint, on its own layer. Reference geometry and labels never export.

    Auto-backup

    Periodically writes timestamped copies of the mechanism into a folder you choose, a set time after the first change since the last backup or save, and prunes the folder to the last few copies. The panel shows the countdown to the next backup. The clock button turns amber when backups are paused waiting for folder access.

    Keyboard shortcuts panel

    Every shortcut in Disjointed comes from one file, and the keyboard button in the File group opens a small panel with three actions. Export writes disjointed-keymap.json — the shortcuts exactly as they are right now. Import… reads one back; tooltips update the moment it loads. Reset returns to the shortcuts Disjointed ships with.

    The file is a keymap/1 document — the format KeyMapper reads, a board that lays every key out at once so you can drag bindings around and see the clashes and the free letters as you go. Export from here, rearrange there, import the result. Your keymap is kept in this browser, not in the mechanism file, so it follows you between mechanisms and not between machines.

    A command can have more than one key (Delete and Backspace both delete) or none at all: about twenty commands ship unbound — Export, the mirrors, the grid and snap toggles, zoom, the theme, Rail, Subtract and Intersect among them — and a keymap can give any of them a key. Esc is hardwired. The shortcuts that answer while you are typing in a field are only Save, Save as, Open and F1. The list in Keyboard shortcuts is the shipped layout.

    On the canvas

    Body

    A rigid part of the mechanism: a filled outline in its own colour. Bodies never bend or stretch; they move as a whole, carried by their joints. Click one to select it and reveal its shape handles; drag it to move it (in Simulate mode, dragging drives the mechanism instead). Drawn with the shape tools in the Body role, recoloured with the colour swatch.

    Grounded body

    A body fixed entirely in the world: it cannot move or turn in simulation, so everything attached to it works against a fixed frame. Made with the Ground tool on the body itself; click it with the tool again to release it.

    Group

    Several bodies (and free joints) that select and move as one and behave as a single rigid body in simulation. Clicking any member selects the whole group, shown with a dashed outline. Made and dissolved with Ctrl+G. Double-click a member to edit the parts inside, with the rest of the drawing faded out.

    Hole

    A cut-out in a body. Holes have the same corner handles and rounding as the outline and are exported with it as inner contours. Round holes can carry a diameter dimension and be repeated by a pattern. Cut with any shape tool in the Cut role (a shape crossing the outline makes a notch instead), or by subtracting a body that lies inside another. Holes don't restrict where joints can go.

    Shape handles

    When a body is selected, small squares mark its control corners and small circles sit on each rounded corner. Drag a square to move the corner (the outline follows), drag a circle along the corner to change its radius, Ctrl-drag a circle to round every corner alike. Double-click an edge to add a corner, double-click a corner to remove it. The same handles appear on holes. A regular polygon shows a centre crosshair and an "n sides" tag instead of letting you add or remove corners.

    Joint

    A point attached to a body. On its own it is just a marked point that moves with the body; it becomes a hinge, an anchor or a slider once a constraint uses it. Its fill tells its role: plain, blue when pinned, yellow when grounded, green when it rides a rail. A joint drawn red with a dashed ring has been stranded outside its body by a shape change — drag it back in. Placed with the Joint tool; delete it and its constraints go with it.

    Free joint

    A joint that belongs to no body, drawn with a dashed ring while nothing holds it. Use one as a fixed anchor by grounding it, as an end of a fixed track, or as a corner to build a body around with the Polyline tool.

    Pin

    Two joints held at the same point while their bodies turn freely about it: the hinge of a linkage, drawn as a blue joint. Made with Connect. Click it with the Weld tool to lock the rotation too.

    Weld

    A pin that also locks the two bodies' relative angle, so they move as one rigid piece while staying separate parts; its centre is drawn square. Made with the Weld tool; clicking a weld with it turns it back into a pin.

    Ground

    A joint fixed to a point in the world, drawn yellow with the ground symbol beneath. The body may still turn about it. Grounds are absolute for the solver: when the rest of the mechanism cannot reach them the assembly is reported as impossible. Made with the Ground tool.

    Rail

    A track between two joints, drawn as a double-headed arrow. When both end joints sit on one body the rail moves with it; when they are free joints it is fixed in the world. Joints placed on it become riders. Its midpoint is a snap and alignment target, and the rail itself takes line constraints and dimensions like a body edge. Drawn with the Rail tool or, together with its carriage, by the Slider tool.

    Rider

    A joint constrained to stay on a rail while its body still turns: a pin in a slot. Drawn green. Click it with the Slider tool to lock its body's orientation, which turns it into a slider.

    Slider

    A rider whose body is also locked to the rail's direction, so the body translates without rotating: a prismatic joint, drawn with a small carriage rectangle. Built in two clicks with the Slider tool; click it with the tool again to unlock the rotation. A slider can be driven by a linear actuator.

    Linear actuator

    A self-driving rail: in animation its carriage travels back and forth between the rail's ends at the configured speed and profile. Select it to edit them in the toolbar's properties strip. Made with the Linear actuator tool; run it with play.

    Motor

    A pivot and a crank pin on one body: in animation the crank pin orbits the pivot at the configured angular speed, turning the body. Select either joint to edit the speed. Made with the Motor tool.

    Reference geometry

    Dash-dot construction elements with a crosshair handle at each defining point: segments and polylines, polygons, circles, arcs and text labels — all finite. They take part in snapping (in preference to the grid), implicit constraints, measurements and constraints, but are not part of the mechanism, are hidden in Simulate mode and never export. Reference geometry is construction: a constraint between it and real geometry moves the reference element first, unless the element is attached to geometry or carries several demands, in which case it becomes the reference and the geometry comes to it — so any number of corners can be held on one line and dragged together. Drag a handle to reshape one (a selected circle shows a rim handle to resize it), drag elsewhere on it to move it whole; select and Delete to remove it. Placed with the reference tools, or any shape tool in the Reference role.

    Dimension

    A measurement between two references with its value on a label. A driven dimension just reports (in parentheses); double-click the label and type a value to make it driving, and the geometry moves to match. Drag the label to reposition it — only the label moves; a point–point dimension's direction is fixed when it is placed and shown by the arrow at the front of the pill, and clicking that arrow cycles it horizontal → vertical → direct. A label dragged past the end of its line gets a dashed leader. In Simulate mode dimensions are live read-outs. See Dimensions and the Measure tool.

    Context dimension

    A temporary, muted dimension between the definition you are editing and the faded context ghost around it. It is never saved and lives only while this definition is open. Double-click its value and type a number to move the live side to that distance once; its direction arrow cycles like a normal dimension's.

    Constraint badge

    The small glyph marking a sketch constraint on an edge, rail, circle or point. Each kind has its own topic: coincident, horizontal, vertical, parallel, perpendicular, equal, fixed, symmetrical, tangential. Hover a badge to see what it constrains, click it to select it, Delete to remove the constraint. A badge drawn red marks a constraint that can't currently hold. The eye on the Constraints group's caption hides all badges. See Sketch constraints.

    Pattern

    One or more holes or joints of a body repeated along one or two directions, or around a centre — a live array: move, reshape or dimension a seed and every instance follows. Drawn in green beside the members: a linear direction shows a dotted line from the seed through every instance, an arrowed spacing label beside the first step and a ×count label past the last; a circular pattern shows a crosshair at the centre with ×count, the angle between instances (even, or degrees) and a ↻ turn / ↑ fixed badge. Double-click any label to type a new value; click a label or the dotted line to select the pattern, then drag the square at the last instance to re-aim it or the centre crosshair to move it. The dotted line is a line reference for constraints and dimensions.

    Instances that would fall outside the body or overlap another feature are ringed red. Delete with the pattern selected to remove the instances and keep the seed; delete a seed to make its instances independent. Made with the linear and circular pattern tools.

    Component instance

    A placed copy of a component definition. Its bodies and joints select as one unit (dashed outline), and every instance follows its definition. Double-click it to edit the definition; Ctrl+double-click to edit it in the context of the surrounding assembly. See Components.

    Context ghost

    The faded drawing of the enclosing assembly shown while a component definition is edited in context. It is inert: it cannot be selected, dragged or constrained, but its corners, midpoints, edges and joints are object-snap targets and the Measure tool can place context dimensions onto it. The breadcrumb eyes choose how many enclosing levels are shown. See Editing a definition in context.

    Getting help

    Click the help button (or press ?) to open this manual beside the canvas. While it is open the pointer is a question mark and every control you click shows its topic here instead of acting; clicking the canvas shows the topic for whatever is drawn there — a body, a hole, a joint, a rail, a badge, a dimension, a pattern, an instance — or, on empty canvas, for the armed tool or the current mode. Close it with the help button, ?, Esc or the ✕ to get back to work. F1 opens the manual at the table of contents. Drag the drawer's left edge to resize it; the arrow button opens the manual in its own tab.
